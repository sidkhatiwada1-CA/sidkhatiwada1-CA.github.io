About me

I spent my first five years in finance doing the work that keeps a business honest: reading statements line by line at Doogar, then building the forecasts that leadership at ICICI actually planned around. That work taught me something specific: the number on the page is rarely the full story. An $8.6% consolidated margin can hide a $3M overvaluation. A clean-looking P&L can mask $1.5M in unrecognized receivables. Good financial analysis means going one layer deeper than the report.

That instinct is what pulled me toward AI and data. If the interesting part of finance is finding the signal buried in the noise, then machine learning is just a more powerful way to do the same job at scale. Training a model to classify sentiment across 4.2 million posts is not a different skill from normalizing an income statement for one-time charges. It's the same question asked with different tools: what does this data actually tell me?

I'm finishing a STEM MS in Finance at Georgia State to formalize that shift, and I hold a Chartered Accountant credential, the international equivalent of a U.S. CPA, from the years before it. Right now I'm looking for an FP&A or financial analyst role where that combination, rigorous accounting judgment plus a comfort with modern data tools, actually matters.

Featured analysis

What is Coty's earnings number actually telling you?

Coty reported a $381M net loss for FY2025. Reading the filing closely tells a different story, and it is the kind of gap an FP&A analyst has to catch before it reaches a forecast.

The question: Coty's headline numbers looked like a company in decline: revenue down 3.7%, operating income cut by more than half, a swing to a net loss. Was the underlying business actually deteriorating, or was the reported number distorted by one-time items?

What I found: Reported operating income fell from $546.7M to $241.1M. But that drop absorbed a $212.8M non-cash impairment charge, $91.8M of restructuring costs, and $70.3M in early termination costs. Stripping those out, normalized EBIT was $616.0M for the year, and gross margin had actually improved for the fifth year running. The business was not falling apart. The reported number was just noisy.

Why it mattered: I built an independent DCF off that normalized EBIT rather than the reported figure, arriving at a base-case value of $7.36 per share against a $2.69 market price. Rather than call the stock simply mispriced, I built a risk-adjusted range down to $2.70 by testing the discount rate the market was implicitly using, and recommended HOLD, since Consumer Beauty's real operating weakness and Coty's thin interest coverage justified some of that gap.

This is the same discipline FP&A work depends on: never plan off the headline number until you know what is driving it.
